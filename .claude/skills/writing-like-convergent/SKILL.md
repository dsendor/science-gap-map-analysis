# Writing like Convergent

Convergent's public writing has a specific voice: **an enthusiastic technical person
telling you what they built, what they are unsure about, and asking you to argue back.**
The corpus is `corpus.md` in this directory — their launch post, their X-Labs posts,
their field and gap descriptions. Read it before a long draft.

The failure this skill exists to fix is not error. It is *shape*: prose that is accurate,
balanced, epigrammatic, and unmistakably not written by a person with a stake in it.
So this is a recipe, not a list of prohibitions. Match the shape.

## The recipe

A piece in their voice has these parts, in this order:

1. **A title, then a one-line subtitle saying what the thing is.**
   *"Introducing the Convergent Research Gap Map / Explore v1.0 of our interactive survey
   of the fundamental development projects landscape."*
2. **An opening that says what you made and where to get it.** Not what you concluded.
   *"Today we're releasing v1.0... You can jump in right away at gap-map.org, and read
   more about where this database came from below."*
3. **A question asked out loud, then answered.** This is their main structural move.
   *"How do you find those pivotal projects?"* · *"So the obvious question is: what's
   left for an X-Lab?"*
4. **The reader in the scene, as "you."** *"After scrolling through a bit, you'll no
   doubt notice..."* · *"If you had better physical and chemical tools, you could
   observe and perturb cells..."*
5. **A scope caveat, early and cheerful, describing the object rather than confessing
   about the author.** *"Think of it and treat it like a crude map of an emerging space
   – not a prioritization of where you should go inside it."*
6. **A close that asks for pushback, names the channels, and means it.**
   *"we're publishing this mostly to get pushback"* · *"Let's explore together!"*
7. **Thanks, by name**, if anyone helped.

## Sentence-level moves

| Move | Their example |
|---|---|
| Contractions, always | "don't fit neatly", "we've built out", "aren't random" |
| Exclamation marks, used sincerely | "(and hopefully provocative!)", "(excitingly!)" |
| Parenthetical self-interruption | "(yes, distinct from Qunnect)", "(notably brains)" |
| Italics on the pivot word | "our aim definitely *isn't* to be comprehensive" |
| A coined compound adjective, reused | "bridge-scale", "FRO-shaped", "X-Lab-shaped" |
| Numbers with physical texture, mid-sentence | "over a mile of cryogenic flex wiring", "1.24 photons of added noise", "about 40,000× higher" |
| Analogy to a canonical project | "what the Human Genome Project catalyzed for biology" |
| Uncertainty as a personal state | "our best current read based on a small number of early conversations", "we want to remain cautious and curious" |
| Long multi-clause sentences that build | see any paragraph of theirs |
| Spaced hyphens and " -- " over em dashes; "&" | "conversation, writing, inquiry, and trial-and-error - and that's exactly what..." |

## The two marks that never survive the first draft

Tested by blind comparison: a draft written straight from the recipe above still came
out with **zero question marks and zero exclamation marks**, and the judge named both
as the largest remaining gap against the real corpus. They are the easiest things to
leave out and the loudest things to leave out.

- **At least one real question mark**, asked of the reader and then answered. It is
  their main structural move and a draft without one is not in their voice, however
  many contractions it has.
- **At least one exclamation mark**, meant sincerely. Their most-quoted line is *"This
  is not by any means a comprehensive survey or prioritized roadmap!"* — the caveat
  itself carries the mark. A page addressed to these readers with none anywhere reads
  as more solemn than its audience.

Count both before shipping. Zero of either is a failed draft.

## The pastiche failure mode

Going the other way is a real failure, not a safe one. **Convergent's casualness always
sits on top of a number.** "about 1 Hz, orders of magnitude too slow." "1.24 photons of
added noise." "over a mile of cryogenic flex wiring."

Casual *and* imprecise is not their register — it is LinkedIn's. Sentences caught in
testing:

- ❌ "let the drafting speedup finally show up in the thing anybody cares about"
  → ✅ "let those researcher-weeks finally show up in time-to-record"
- ❌ "a number you can put on a dashboard" — corporate-analytics vocabulary, aimed at
  people who fund telescopes
- ❌ "agreeing what a review *even* means" — conversational intensifier carrying nothing
- ❌ "and gets no error *at all*" — padding a sentence that was stronger short

Rule: every loosened sentence must land on something countable. If you relax the
register and the precision at the same time, tighten one of them back.

## What the default voice does instead

Read your draft for these. Each one is the tell.

- **The epigram.** A short declarative closing a paragraph, engineered to land.
  *"They do."* · *"Publishing did not get cheaper."* · *"The step that most needs a
  measure is the one with none."* Convergent never writes these. Fold the point back
  into the sentence that earned it.
- **The antithesis pair.** *"Your gap names what is wrong. The outcome names what is on
  the other side."* Two clauses of matched length pivoting on a contrast. One per
  document at most.
- **Bolded lead-ins on every bullet.** They bold rarely and never in a column.
- **The confession block.** A paragraph of caveats in the formal register, placed to
  discharge an obligation. Their caveats are one cheerful sentence about the artifact.
- **Uniform rhythm.** Every paragraph three-to-four sentences, every sentence
  fifteen-to-twenty-five words. Theirs run from four words to sixty.
- **Abstract nouns doing the verbs' work.** *"the labor the saving displaced landed
  downstream."* Say who did what to whom.
- **No humor, no asides, no enthusiasm.** If nothing in the draft could have been cut
  by an editor for being too excited, it is not in their voice.

## Two things to keep from the house rules

Their voice is warm but not vague, and it is not a licence to loosen anything else:

- **The claim still comes first and stays one claim.** Progressive disclosure is
  unchanged; only the register changes.
- **Never adopt their "we" for their map.** They wrote "we" about work they did. In our
  documents, "you" is Convergent and "I" is the author. The enthusiasm is ours to
  borrow; the authorship is not.

## Check before shipping

Count, then read. The counts catch the omissions; the read catches the pastiche.

| Count | Target |
|---|---|
| Question marks | ≥ 1, asked of the reader |
| Exclamation marks | ≥ 1, meant sincerely |
| Contractions | ≥ 20 per 1,200 words (a tested draft in their voice hit 29) |
| One-sentence paragraphs used as beats | 0 |
| Sentence length range | wide — 4 to 60 words, not 15 to 25 |
| Loosened sentences with no number in them | 0 |

Then read it aloud. If it sounds like a person who has spent a weekend inside someone
else's data and wants to argue about it, it is right. If it sounds like a
well-organized report, go back to the recipe.

## Test it the way this skill was tested

Give both drafts to a subagent with the identifying headers stripped, labelled A and B,
and have it fetch the corpus URLs itself rather than trusting a summary. Ask two
questions kept separate — *which reads as more machine-written* and *which sounds more
like Convergent* — since they can diverge, and require quoted sentences for every claim.
Ask it to name the pastiche overshoots explicitly; a judge told only to find AI tells
will not volunteer them.
